PHV AI Assistant

Architecture & Design Document

Personal Health Vault • April 2026 • v0.1 Draft

1. Vision

The AI Assistant extends the Personal Health Vault with an intelligent, conversational interface to a patient's complete health record. Rather than requiring the user to navigate timelines and search results manually, the assistant allows natural questions ("How has my kidney function changed since the hospitalization?"), proactive insights ("Your heart rate has declined 18% since treatment started — this is commonly reported with bispecific antibody therapy"), and multi-model clinical debates ("Let's get a second opinion on this finding").

The assistant is not a physician and does not replace professional medical advice. It is a tool for health literacy, pattern recognition, and informed conversation with one's care team.

2. Design Principles

Local-First Data SovereigntyAll patient data, chat history, summaries, and insights remain on the user's machine. Nothing is stored by LLM providers. The user owns and controls every artifact.
Provider PortabilityThe architecture is model-agnostic. Conversations, patient summaries, and insights are stored in provider-neutral formats. Switching from Claude to GPT to a local model requires only a configuration change.
Narrative Over StructureLLMs reason best over natural language. Raw FHIR data is transformed into clinical prose before being presented to any model. The AI reads a patient chart, not a database.
Proactive, Not Just ReactiveThe system doesn't just answer questions — it actively looks for correlations, anomalies, and clinically significant patterns, surfacing insights before the user thinks to ask.
Cumulative Medical MemoryThe patient record is append-only. Past findings are never discarded, because today's irrelevant detail may be tomorrow's critical clue. Context is accumulated, not compressed away.
Transparent ReasoningEvery insight cites its evidence. Every summary can be traced back to source data. The user can always inspect what data the AI saw and how it reached its conclusions.
Content AttributionEvery entry in the patient chart carries provenance: who produced it (physician, lab system, AI actor, or user) and what kind of content it is (fact vs. reasoning). Tiers are defined by the nature of the content, not by its author. A physician's factual report and an AI-generated factual summary both belong in Tier 1; a physician's clinical reasoning and an AI-generated inference both belong in Tier 3. Attribution determines the weight and trust level the reader — human or AI — should assign to each entry.

4. The Patient Chart (Layered Document)

The patient chart is the central knowledge artifact. It transforms raw FHIR data into clinical narrative that LLMs can reason over effectively. It is stored as local files (Markdown or structured JSON), versioned, and fully under the user's control.

Tier 1 — Chronological Medical Narrative

What it is: An append-only, chronologically ordered document that captures every clinically significant fact: diagnoses, hospitalizations, medication changes, procedures, and notable lab findings. Think of it as the "chart notes" a physician maintains. Entries may be generated by any source — extracted from a physician's report, summarized from incoming FHIR data by the Narrator AI, or entered directly by the user — but all Tier 1 content is factual in nature: what happened, when, and what was measured.

Why append-only: Today's minor finding may be tomorrow's critical clue. The MGUS diagnosed in 2020 appeared unremarkable until it became central to a myeloma investigation five years later. SIBO diagnosed years ago is relevant to current GI investigations. By never compressing or discarding, the narrative preserves the full clinical thread.

Size: Clinical events are far fewer than individual data points. Even a complex medical history spanning decades fits in a few pages of prose — well within any modern context window.

Example:

## 2020

**March 2020** — Routine labs at Stanford revealed a monoclonal protein
(IgG kappa) on SPEP. M-protein measured at 0.3 g/dL. Free kappa/lambda ratio
mildly elevated at 2.1. Diagnosed as MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of
undetermined significance). Recommended annual monitoring.

**June 2020** — Diagnosed with SIBO (small intestinal bacterial overgrowth)
following hydrogen breath test. Treated with rifaximin 550mg TID x 14 days.
Symptoms (bloating, irregular bowel habits) partially resolved.
Tier 2 — Metric Summaries (Auto-Generated)

What it is: For each lab metric tracked in the timeline, a concise narrative paragraph summarizing the trend: phases of stability, inflection points, notable excursions, and current status. These are regenerated automatically whenever new data arrives.

How it's generated: The system detects phases in each metric (stable, trending up/down, volatile), computes summary statistics per phase, identifies inflection points, and generates prose. This is a deterministic + LLM hybrid: statistical analysis identifies the patterns, then a fast LLM (Haiku-class) renders them as clinical narrative.

Disposable: These summaries can always be regenerated from raw FHIR data. They exist to compress thousands of data points into a few sentences that fit in a conversation context.

Example:

**Creatinine (LOINC 2160-0, Serum)** — 117 measurements, 2010–2026.
Stable at 0.9–1.1 mg/dL (mean 1.0) from 2010 through July 2025.
Spiked to 2.8 mg/dL during August 2025 hospitalization (acute kidney
injury, likely treatment-related). Gradually normalized: 1.8 by Sep,
1.3 by Nov, 1.2 by Jan 2026. Current baseline appears reset ~0.2
higher than pre-hospitalization. Reference range: 0.7–1.3 mg/dL.
Tier 3 — Clinical Context Notes

What it is: Annotations that capture reasoning, connections, and clinical context that aren't obvious from raw data alone. The defining characteristic is interpretive content: not what happened, but what it means, why it matters, and how findings connect to each other or to medical knowledge. Tier 3 content comes from multiple sources and each entry carries explicit attribution.

Sources and attribution: A single incoming medical record may produce both Tier 1 and Tier 3 content. The physician's factual report ("M-protein measured at 0.3 g/dL") goes to Tier 1; the physician's clinical reasoning ("recommend monitoring for progression given prior MGUS diagnosis") goes to Tier 3 with attribution source: physician. When the Analyst AI later notices a trend and writes "FLC ratio trending upward over three quarters — may warrant reassessment of MGUS stability," that goes to Tier 3 with source: analyst. When the user adds personal context ("I stopped taking metoprolol in 2023 because of side effects"), that goes to Tier 3 with source: user. Attribution matters because it determines the weight each note carries: a physician's assessment has clinical authority, the Analyst's inference is a prompt to investigate, and the user's annotation is personal context no one else has.

Examples: "The MGUS diagnosed in 2020 should be monitored for progression to multiple myeloma. Key markers to track: M-protein level, free light chain ratio, hemoglobin, calcium, creatinine, and bone lesions." [source: physician, 2020-03] — Or: "Patient reports that beta-blocker (metoprolol) was discontinued in 2023 — this may explain the upward BP trend observed in the timeline." [source: analyst, 2026-01]

Persistence: These notes accumulate over time. They are the system's institutional memory — the equivalent of a physician's clinical knowledge about a specific patient.

5. AI Actors

The system has five distinct AI actors, each with a different role, trigger, and model preference.

The Narrator

Role: Transforms raw FHIR data into the patient chart's narrative tiers. Detects trends, phases, and inflection points. Renders clinical prose.

Trigger: Runs on data ingestion (new labs arrive) or on schedule (daily/weekly refresh).

Model preference: Fast and cheap (Haiku-class). The task is well-defined: data in, narrative out. Doesn't require deep reasoning.

  • Generates Tier 2 metric summaries
  • Proposes updates to Tier 1 narrative
  • Applies statistical analysis (phase detection, trend fitting)
The Analyst

Role: Proactively searches for correlations, anomalies, and clinically significant patterns. The "curious physician" who reviews the chart looking for connections.

Trigger: Scheduled (after each Narrator run) and on-demand. Can also be triggered when the user asks "anything new?"

Model preference: Mid-tier (Sonnet-class). Needs medical knowledge and reasoning ability, but tasks are bounded and structured.

  • Correlation pass: metrics that change together
  • Deviation pass: baselines that shift
  • Association pass: events ↔ metric changes
  • Literature-informed interpretation
  • Generates Tier 3 clinical context notes
  • May ask the user clarifying questions
The Assistant

Role: Conversational interface. Answers the user's questions about their health data, explains findings, and helps prepare for doctor's appointments.

Trigger: User-initiated (chat messages).

Model preference: Configurable by user. Defaults to high-capability model (Opus/Sonnet-class) for nuanced medical reasoning.

  • Reads the full patient chart as context
  • Can request additional FHIR queries for specific data
  • Surfaces relevant Analyst insights
  • Cites evidence for all claims
  • Respects medical disclaimer boundaries
The Debate Arena

Role: Facilitates multi-model clinical discussions. Two or more models review the same evidence and offer independent perspectives, much like consulting multiple specialists.

Trigger: User-initiated ("get a second opinion") or triggered on high-confidence Analyst insights.

Model preference: Two or more different providers/models. The value comes from diversity of reasoning.

  • Round-robin with user as moderator
  • Same patient context given to each model
  • Models see each other's prior responses
  • Visually distinct in the UI (colors/avatars per model)
  • User can interject, redirect, or conclude
The Sentinel

Role: Monitors external medical sources for content relevant to the patient's specific conditions, medications, and concerns. Bridges the local health record with evolving medical knowledge.

Trigger: Scheduled (configurable: daily to weekly). Also runs on-demand and after significant chart changes (new diagnosis, new medication).

Model preference: Two-stage: Haiku-class for relevance filtering, Sonnet-class for contextualization and Tier 3 note drafting.

  • Derives search terms from the patient chart automatically
  • Queries PubMed, FDA alerts, ClinicalTrials.gov, guideline repositories
  • Scores results for patient-specific relevance (not just topic match)
  • Generates Tier 3 notes with source: literature or source: guideline
  • User-configurable aggressiveness and source selection

6. The Analyst in Detail

The Active Analyst is the most novel component. It performs four analytical passes over the patient's data, each looking for different kinds of patterns.

6.1 Correlation Pass

Examines pairs of metrics over time windows and asks: did anything change together? Uses statistical correlation (Pearson/Spearman) on time-aligned metric series as a first filter, then sends significant correlations to the LLM for clinical interpretation.

Example output: "Heart rate (mean) declined from 78 to 64 bpm over Oct–Dec 2025. This 18% reduction coincides temporally with the initiation of teclistamab (first dose Oct 3, 2025). In the oncology literature, heart rate reduction after bispecific antibody therapy is commonly reported and may reflect decreased tumor burden and associated inflammatory response."

6.2 Deviation Pass

Examines each metric independently for baseline shifts — not just out-of-range values, but subtle drifts that are still technically normal. Uses change-point detection algorithms (e.g., PELT or Bayesian change-point) to identify when a metric's distribution shifts.

Example output: "Fasting glucose has been trending upward since mid-2024, with a mean shift from 88 to 96 mg/dL. While still within the normal range (70–100), this trajectory, if continued, would cross into pre-diabetic territory (~100 mg/dL) within approximately 12 months."

6.3 Association Pass

Connects events (medication starts/stops, hospitalizations, diagnoses) to metric changes using temporal association. When a new medication starts, it monitors subsequent labs for statistically significant shifts within a clinically relevant time window.

Example output: "Potassium has averaged 3.6 mmol/L since February 2026, down from a historical mean of 4.2. This coincides with the addition of furosemide (started Jan 28, 2026). Diuretic-induced hypokalemia is a well-known side effect. Consider discussing with your physician."

6.4 Literature-Informed Interpretation

Takes the statistical findings from the passes above and contextualizes them with medical knowledge. This is where the LLM's training on medical literature adds value: distinguishing expected treatment effects from concerning side effects, identifying patterns consistent with disease progression, and flagging findings that warrant clinical attention.

User interaction model: The Analyst doesn't just produce findings — it engages the user. It may ask: "I see your creatinine baseline shifted after the August hospitalization. Has your nephrologist commented on this, or should I flag it for your next appointment?" Or: "I'm generating a summary of your protein electrophoresis trends. Can you confirm that the myeloma diagnosis was established in [date]?" This keeps the user in the loop and improves accuracy.

7. Trigger Architecture & Scheduling

The proactive AI actors need to be triggered — they don't just respond to user questions. The system supports four distinct trigger patterns, managed by a background task runner in the Docker Compose stack (e.g., APScheduler within FastAPI or a dedicated worker service).

7.1 Event-Driven Triggers

When new FHIR data is ingested — a MyChart sync, a manual upload, a lab feed — a post-ingestion hook fires a processing pipeline: Narrator first (update Tiers 1 and 2), then Analyst (run passes on changed metrics), then Sentinel (check whether new diagnoses or medications warrant new web watch terms). This is the highest-value trigger because the data is fresh and the user is most likely to care about what changed.

7.2 Scheduled Triggers

A configurable scheduler (default: daily) runs time-aware checks even when no new data has arrived. Time itself is clinically meaningful: "It's been 90 days since your last CBC and your previous pattern was quarterly." "Your M-protein was last checked 6 months ago — MGUS guidelines suggest annual monitoring." "Creatinine has been in the post-hospitalization recovery band for 4 months — worth checking if it's stabilized." The Sentinel's web monitoring also runs on schedule — scanning medical literature, FDA alerts, and guideline updates for content relevant to the patient's conditions and medications.

7.3 Presentation Triggers

When the user opens the Assistant tab, the system surfaces unread insights that were generated in the background by the other triggers. Before the user asks anything, the Assistant can say: "Since we last talked, I noticed your free light chain ratio has moved from 1.8 to 2.4 over the last two results. Still within range, but the trend is worth watching." Or: "A new study on MGUS progression markers was published last week — here's how it relates to your monitoring." The insight store acts as a buffer between background analysis and user-facing presentation, decoupling when analysis runs from when the user sees results.

7.4 User-Initiated Triggers

The user can explicitly ask "anything new?" or "run a full analysis" to trigger on-demand Analyst and Sentinel passes. The Debate Arena is always user-initiated.

Cost awareness: The schedule should be user-configurable — daily, weekly, or on-demand only. Cloud LLM calls have real costs; the Sentinel's web searches consume API quota. A local model (via Ollama) changes this calculus entirely, enabling aggressive scheduling at no marginal cost. The UI should show estimated cost per analysis cycle based on current provider and model settings.

8. The Sentinel (Web Intelligence)

The Sentinel is a vigilant, proactive actor that continuously monitors external medical sources for content relevant to the patient's specific situation. Like a sentinel node in medicine — the first to detect change — it stands watch over the evolving landscape of medical knowledge and alerts the system when something matters to this patient.

8.1 Watch List Derivation

The Sentinel reads the patient chart and automatically derives search terms: active diagnoses, current medications, relevant LOINC codes, recent procedures, and flagged concerns from Tier 3 notes. For example, a patient with MGUS, on teclistamab, with recent kidney function changes would generate watch terms like "MGUS progression," "teclistamab long-term outcomes," "creatinine recovery post-bispecific antibody therapy," and "free light chain ratio monitoring guidelines." The watch list updates automatically as the chart evolves.

8.2 Sources

The Sentinel queries structured medical sources on a configurable schedule:

  • PubMed / MEDLINE — new research relevant to the patient's conditions and treatments
  • FDA Safety Communications — drug safety alerts, recalls, black box warning updates
  • ClinicalTrials.gov — new trials relevant to the patient's condition and demographics
  • Clinical guideline repositories (NCCN, ASCO, AHA, etc.) — updated screening intervals, treatment protocols, diagnostic criteria
  • Drug interaction databases — newly discovered interactions for the patient's current medication regimen
  • General medical news — filtered through a relevance threshold to avoid noise

8.3 Relevance Scoring

Raw search results are filtered through a two-stage process. First, a fast model (Haiku-class) scores each result for patient-specific relevance — not just "is this about myeloma" but "is this relevant to this patient's stage, markers, treatment history, and current concerns." Results above a configurable threshold advance to the second stage, where a mid-tier model (Sonnet-class) reads the abstract or summary, contextualizes it against the patient chart, and drafts a Tier 3 clinical context note with source: literature or source: guideline attribution, linked to the source URL and retrieval date.

8.4 User Control

The user controls the Sentinel's aggressiveness through several settings: which source categories are active, how frequently each is checked, the relevance threshold for surfacing findings, and whether to include general medical news or limit to structured databases. Some users want every new PubMed abstract touching their conditions; others want only FDA alerts and guideline changes. The default is conservative — high-confidence, high-relevance findings only.

Attribution example: A Sentinel finding might appear as: "A phase III trial (NCT05xxxxx) published in Blood (March 2026) reports 5-year outcomes for teclistamab in relapsed myeloma, showing durable responses in patients who achieved CR by 12 months. Your last response assessment was [date]." [source: literature, retrieved 2026-04-06, PubMed ID: xxxxxxxx]

9. The Debate Arena

Inspired by the experience of having multiple specialists review the same case, the Debate Arena allows two or more AI models to independently assess the same clinical question, then respond to each other's reasoning.

How it works

The user poses a question or selects an Analyst insight to debate. The Debate Manager sends the same patient context (chart + relevant data) to Model A. Model A's response is then included in the context sent to Model B, along with the instruction to provide an independent assessment and note any areas of agreement or disagreement. The process can continue for additional rounds or additional models. The user can interject at any point to redirect, ask follow-ups, or conclude.

Why different models matter

Different LLMs have different training data, different reasoning tendencies, and different blind spots. A finding that one model dismisses as routine might be flagged as significant by another. This diversity of perspective is exactly what makes multi-specialist consultation valuable in medicine — and it translates directly to the multi-model setting.

UI presentation

Each model gets a distinct visual identity (color, icon, name). The conversation flows vertically with clear attribution. The user's messages are visually centered, with model responses appearing on alternating sides or in color-coded blocks. A summary panel can highlight points of agreement and disagreement.

11. Provider Abstraction

Each LLM provider implements a common interface:

class LLMProvider:
    async def send_message(
        messages: list[Message],      # conversation history
        system_prompt: str,           # role definition + medical context
        model: str,                   # specific model ID
        stream: bool = True,          # stream tokens for real-time UI
        temperature: float = 0.3,     # low for clinical accuracy
    ) -> AsyncIterator[str]:
        """Send messages and yield response tokens."""
        ...

    def format_messages(
        messages: list[Message],      # provider-neutral format
    ) -> list[dict]:
        """Convert to provider-specific message format."""
        ...

Provider adapters translate between the neutral internal format (role + content + metadata) and each provider's API format. The orchestrator never sees provider-specific details.

11.1 Deployment Model: Local / Cloud Split

The current hardware is a MacBook Air M4 with 32GB unified memory, running the full Docker stack (PostgreSQL, OpenSearch, HAPI FHIR, FastAPI, web server) at roughly 3–4GB. This leaves 20–24GB of headroom after macOS overhead — more than enough to run local models alongside the stack.

The principle: run locally what you can, use cloud APIs for what demands frontier-model reasoning. This minimizes cost, maximizes data sovereignty, and keeps the most frequent operations (Narrator runs on every data ingest) free of API fees and external network dependency.

12. Implementation Roadmap

Phase 1: Foundation (MVP)

Single-model conversational assistant with Claude. Basic patient chart (auto-generated Tier 2 metric summaries). Chat history in SQLite. New "Assistant" tab in the web UI. Medical disclaimer.

Phase 2: The Narrator, Patient Chart & Local Models

Ollama integration with a local 8B model (Llama 3 or Mistral) running natively on macOS. Automated Tier 1 narrative generation from existing FHIR data, powered by the local model at zero API cost. Tier 2 metric summaries with statistical phase detection. User-editable Tier 3 clinical context notes. Chart versioning. This phase establishes the local-first pattern from the start rather than treating it as a late addition.

Phase 3: The Analyst

Correlation and deviation detection passes (cloud, Sonnet-class). Association pass (events to metrics). Insight store with evidence references. Insight feed in the UI. User interaction loop (questions and confirmations). Background task runner with event-driven and scheduled triggers.

Phase 4: The Sentinel (Web Intelligence)

Automatic watch list derivation from patient chart. PubMed and FDA alert monitoring. Two-stage relevance pipeline: Stage 1 filtering on local model (zero cost, high throughput), Stage 2 contextualization on cloud (Sonnet-class, low volume). Tier 3 note generation with literature/guideline attribution. Configurable schedule, sources, and aggressiveness. ClinicalTrials.gov integration for relevant trial matching.

Phase 5: Provider Abstraction & Debate

OpenAI and Google provider adapters. Model selector in the UI. Debate Arena with multi-model round-robin. Visual differentiation of model responses.

Phase 6: Advanced Local & Offline

Larger local models (13–14B) for improved Narrator quality. Full offline mode for all non-debate operations. Model performance comparison tooling. Aggressive Sentinel scheduling at zero marginal cost. Evaluation framework to continuously assess whether local models can take over more roles as they improve.

13. Security & Privacy Considerations

Patient health data is among the most sensitive personal information. The architecture addresses this through several mechanisms.

Data locality: All patient data, chat history, and derived artifacts stay on the user's machine. LLM API calls transmit conversation context (which includes health data) to the provider, but nothing is stored on the provider's side when using API access (as opposed to consumer chat products).

API-only access: The system uses API endpoints (not consumer chat interfaces) for all LLM providers. API usage typically has stronger data handling guarantees — Anthropic's API terms, for example, state that API inputs and outputs are not used for model training.

Local model option: For maximum privacy, the Ollama integration allows fully offline operation with no data leaving the machine at all. This comes at the cost of model capability but may be preferred for certain queries.

Minimal context principle: The context builder should include only the patient data relevant to the current question, not the entire chart, when possible. The Tier 2 summaries serve this purpose — they compress thousands of data points into a few sentences, minimizing the health data transmitted per request.

Important note on medical disclaimer: The UI must prominently display that this system is not a medical device, does not provide medical advice, and should not be used as a substitute for professional medical judgment. The assistant's responses should include appropriate hedging ("you may want to discuss this with your physician") and never make definitive diagnostic or treatment claims.
